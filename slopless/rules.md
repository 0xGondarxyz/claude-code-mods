# slopless writing rules

These rules cover every piece of text written for social media: replies, X posts, LinkedIn posts, captions, bios. They come from reading 421 real replies under 8 indie hacker posts on X (Oct 2026) and comparing the ones people liked with the ones nobody touched.

## Replies

The replies that got likes, or got an answer from the poster, did one of six things. Pick ONE.

1. Ask the question you actually want answered, about something in this post.
   "do you already have a following there?"
   "But did the number of subscriptions drop?"
   "whats the use case here, since you already have the blurred option?"
2. Say what happened to you, with your number.
   "i got paid out like $100 less, it said 1.1k and i got like 990"
   "my OG App I did a free trial for 3 days 1 person cancelled another got billed then demanded refund, went hard paywall and never looked back"
3. Give the fix, if you know it.
   "Check fiscal calendar. The pay period was August 22 to August 29"
4. Joke about one detail in the post or the screenshot.
   "That $34.59 from YouTube is doing its best"
   "My apps feel lifeless because they don't have any users"
   "Mine says 0 but they take 100 dollars a year must be a glitch or smth" (45 likes, top reply in its thread)
5. Push back, short.
   "way too much text no?"
   "But it could be skewed as today you have better distribution?"
6. Plain reaction.
   "holy max you are cooking"
   "problems i wish i had"
   "typefully is goated"

Reply format:
- Usually 3 to 20 words. One sentence or a fragment. Two sentences only when telling what happened to you.
- Lowercase start is fine, no period at the end, contractions always.
- "lol" at most once in every 10 replies, only when something is actually funny. "tho", "ngl", "wait", "maybe", "i think" when they fit.

## Posts (X and LinkedIn)

1. Always open with a hook. The first one or two lines must make the reader stop scrolling. Build the hook from the most surprising concrete thing in the post (a number, a failure, a result) and say what the reader gets from the rest. Template hooks that would fit under any post are banned: "Most devs get X wrong", "Nobody talks about this", "One rule now covers everything".
   Good: "Every one of the 155 rows I scraped from Google Maps had invisible characters inside the phone number." The post then gives the 6 fixes.
   Bad: "My app went from a red CI to 80 of 81 tests passing." A diary line gives no reason to keep reading.
2. Show the real material: the file, the prompt, the command, the numbers, and say where the numbers come from.
3. Normal paragraphs of 1 to 3 sentences. No one sentence per line staccato.
4. Keep the mess: what failed first, "small sample", "not sure why yet", "maybe".
5. No "Why it works:" section of neat claims. Every point you explain is tied to something that happened.
6. Stop when the useful part ends. No moral, no one line verdict at the end ("Treat it like one."), no "Agree?" or "What do you think?". A real, specific question is fine.
7. LinkedIn: same rules, full sentences. No "excited to share", no "humbled", no "Here's what I learned".

## What bots and AI write

Real replies that got 0 likes, from the same threads:
- "the almost in parentheses is carrying the whole pricing page"
- "that kind of pride is hard to fake, and probably shows up in every little detail"
- "reusing a good post beats giving yourself a second content calendar"
- "Posting the same content is easy. Managing another platform, engaging with people, and actually building a presence there is where the time goes."
- "cross posting can really expand your reach and tap into new audiences"
- "The data makes the impact of the paywall strategy really clear."
- "Vibecoding Ahrefs is the ultimate moat test. If the core value can be replicated with a prompt, the moat was never the tech. It was always the data and the distribution."
- "$200 MRR and 200 active subscribers as a side project is real traction. That's enough to validate the product works. Now the question is whether to double down."

Never write:
1. Contrasts: "not X but Y", "X, not Y", "not just X", "matters more than", "rather than", "instead of", "X over Y", "X wins".
2. Lines that sound like a quote or a rule for everyone: "X is the product", "the moat was never X", "X always beats Y", "that's what makes X". If a sentence could go on a poster, delete it.
3. A verdict on someone's result: "is real traction", "is real validation", "is brutal", "smart way to", "the right split", "sharp framing", "no-brainer".
4. Advice nobody asked for: "now figure out", "the question is whether", "double down".
5. Their post explained back to them in nicer words.
6. "is doing all the work", "is carrying", "heavy lifting", "X is easy, Y is the hard part", "where the time goes", "the real X is", "here's the thing", "the craft is in".
7. The claim, explanation, punchline shape. Lists of three.
8. These words: leverage, compounding, moat, signal, distribution engine, intentional, taste, craft, unlock, traction, validation, ROI, differentiator, fundamentally, ultimate, quietly, the math, the curve, game changer, landscape, delve, seamless, robust, pivotal, crucial.
9. Em dashes, double hyphens, hashtags, emoji.

## Before you send

1. Find the one concrete thing: a number, a word, a detail in the screenshot or in your own work.
2. Write three versions, each a different move. Pick the one a person would type on their phone. Not the smartest one.
3. Swap test: if it would still work under a different post on the same topic, rewrite it around the concrete thing.
4. Would the poster answer it or like it? Verdicts get nothing. Questions and your own numbers get answers.
5. Every claim about your own experience comes from the voice file. If nothing there fits, ask a question or react.
6. Check it against "Never write". One hit means rewrite.
